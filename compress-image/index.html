What compression actually does

A photo file holds far more detail than most screens ever show. Formats like JPG and WebP use that: when a picture is saved, they keep the detail your eyes notice most and let go of the rest. That is why a JPG can be a tenth the size of the same picture stored exactly, pixel for pixel, the way PNG stores it.

Compressing re-saves the picture at the quality level you choose. A lower quality lets go of more detail, so the file gets smaller. The picture's dimensions do not change — a 4,000-pixel-wide photo stays 4,000 pixels wide. Only the weight of the file changes.

Choosing the right quality

  • 90 to 100 — barely smaller. Worth it only when every detail matters, like a photo you plan to print large.
  • 80 to 90 — the sweet spot for most photos. Files shrink a lot, and on a screen the difference from the original is hard to spot.
  • 60 to 79 — noticeably smaller again. Good for previews, thumbnails and quick shares where the picture is seen small.
  • Below 60 — use with care. Faces stay readable, but skies and smooth gradients can turn blocky.

Why your photo is never uploaded

Most compressors work on a server: you upload your photo, wait, then download the result and trust that the copy on their machine gets deleted. Here the work happens in your browser, on your device, using the same technology that displays this page. There is no server copy, so there is nothing to delete and nothing to leak. It is also why the tool is instant and has no daily limit.

Common problems and fixes

  • Barely smaller than before — the file is probably a PNG photo, or a picture that was already compressed once, for example one saved from a social app. PNG has no quality setting; convert it to JPG or WebP first, using the converter tools on this site.
  • Looks blurry or blocky — the quality went too low for a picture that will be viewed large. Raise the quality, or resize the picture smaller at a higher quality instead; a smaller picture at 85 often looks better than a big one at 50.
  • The result is bigger than the original — the original was already stored efficiently, most often as WebP. In that case keep the original; compression cannot beat a file that is already tight.

Questions

Is it free to compress images here?

Yes — free, no account, no watermark, no daily limit.

Will my photo look worse?

At 80–90% quality most people cannot see a difference. Go lower only when a form has a strict size limit.

Are my images uploaded to be compressed?

No. Compression happens in your browser, on your device. That is also why it is instant.

Need an exact size, like under 200 KB?

Use our Compress to a Target Size tool — it adjusts everything for you until the file fits.

Can I compress a photo without losing any quality at all?

Partly. A PNG can be re-saved with no detail lost, but a photo stored that way stays large. For JPG and WebP, a smaller file always means some detail was let go — at 80 to 90 quality it is detail most eyes cannot see on a screen.

How many times can I compress the same photo?

As many as you like, but always start again from the original. Every re-save at a lower quality lets go of a little more detail, and the losses add up. Keep the original file untouched and make each compressed copy from it.
